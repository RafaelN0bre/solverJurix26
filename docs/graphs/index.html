Fairchild v Glenhaven (2002) — Overdetermination

The claimant was exposed to asbestos at three employers (Ex1, Ex2, Ex3); any *two* of the three exposures are, on their own, sufficient to cause the cancer (Ca), which defeasibly causes death (Di). Since the three sufficient pairs overlap, no single exposure is individually necessary for Di -- yet the paper's cause-in-fact test still counts each Ex_i as a cause, unlike the stricter but-for test.

Justified conclusions: Ca, Di, Ex1, Ex2, Ex3

People v. Dlugash (1997) — Preemption

Bush (BuSh) and Dlugash (DlSh) both shoot the victim. Bush's shot kills first; because Geller was already dead, Dlugash's shot could not, as a matter of fact, have killed him -- r4 undercuts r1's applicability. Bush's shooting is a cause of Geller's death (GeDi); Dlugash's is not.

Justified conclusions: BuKi, BuSh, DlSh, GeDi, ¬r1

Cassazione penale (2023) — Omission

The child had leukaemia (ChLe) and the parents refused consent to chemotherapy (¬PaCo); the child died (ChDi). ChLe is a plain NESS cause (it is literally in the sufficient set). ¬PaCo is not -- it never appears as a premise of any argument for ChDi -- yet axiom-expanding with PaCo would enable Chem, which would undercut r0 (Definition 4.3, case 3), so the omission is still a cause under the paper's full notion.

Justified conclusions: ChDi, ChLe, ¬PaCo

Saunders v Adams (1928) — Preemptive negative causation

The brakes had a pre-existing malfunction (BrMa) and the driver did not push the brake pedal in time (¬DrPu); the accident happened (AcHa). The driver's omission also undercuts the malfunction's causal chain (r3: since the pedal was never pushed, whether the faulty brakes would have failed is never actually tested), so ¬DrPu is a cause of AcHa but BrMa is not.

Justified conclusions: AcHa, BrMa, ¬DrPu, ¬r2
